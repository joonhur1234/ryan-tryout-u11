1st Period

Understand the assignment

The Prospects Game brings the top-performing skaters from tryouts together to play head-to-head. Coaches use it to compare players directly under real game speed, tighter space, and faster decisions — it's how the final spots on the top roster get decided.

Getting the invite means the evaluators already like what they've seen. It's not a finish line. Effort, attitude, and hockey sense in this specific game still carry real weight in the final call.

The mindset that works
Treat it like any other good game, not a test. Players who play loose and hard get noticed more than players who look like they're trying not to make a mistake.
Format
Full-ice game with real referees and a running clock.
Shifts
Short and timed — 45 to 60 second buzzers, no long shifts.
Lines
Expect new linemates. Evaluators want to see adaptability.
Evaluators
In the stands scoring habits and detail — not just goals and points.

How tryout jamboree games work

This phase is built for repetition and comparison. Short games and frequent rotations give evaluators more chances to see how Ryan competes, communicates, and adapts.

01

Mini-game structure

Instead of a full-length game, teams or split squads play shortened periods, such as two 10-minute halves or one 15-minute quarter.

02

Round-robin rotation

Players rotate across multiple short matches so evaluators can see them with different teammates and against different opponents.

03

High repetition

Rules may be tweaked to fast-track situational play, such as starting possessions at the 20-yard line or taking immediate free throws, so the action keeps moving.

Forward Positioning by Zone

1

Offensive Zone (O-Zone)

Stay high enough to support the puck, but low enough to protect the middle. Create a triangle with the puck carrier and the weak-side forward so the defense has to guard multiple lanes at once.

  • Attack the open lane instead of drifting to the wall.
  • Read the defender's hips and attack the weak side.
  • Drive hard to the net when a winger has the puck.
2

Defensive Zone (D-Zone)

Take good angles and force the play wide. Keep your stick active, take away the middle, and be the first forward back to help win the puck or at least limit the passing lane.

  • Pick the inside lane and protect the slot.
  • Backcheck through the middle instead of chasing behind.
  • Win the battle first, then look for the breakout.

💡 Pro Tips for U11 Rep Forwards

  • Full speed is a weapon, not a default setting. Use it at the right moments to create pressure and separation.
  • Controlled skating builds deception. A sharper angle and stronger body position beats nonstop speed.
  • When your feet are moving, make decisions quickly. The best U11 forwards are efficient, not just explosive.

Breakout Rule

When your defenseman wins the puck

When your defenseman (D-man) wins the battle and gets the puck while the other team is attacking, your team switches from defense to offense. This is called a breakout, and it requires the forwards to move immediately to their specific exit spots.

The Breakout Responsibilities

Strong-Side Winger
  • Get to the wall: Immediately sprint down to the boards and face the D-man near the hashmarks / breakout zone.
  • Open up your body: Put your back flat against the boards so you can see the whole ice. Do not look away from the D-man.
  • Be a target: Get your stick down on the ice to give your D-man a clean target for a pass.
Center
  • Curl low and slow: Do not run away up the ice. Instead, skate low toward your own goal line and swing through the middle of the ice (the “dot line”) to support the D-man.
  • Be the backup option: If the D-man cannot pass to the winger on the boards, they will pass it into the middle to you.
Weak-Side Winger
  • Stretch the ice: Move up toward the blue line near the center of the ice or the opposite boards.
  • Be the next target: Once the Strong-Side Winger or Center gets the puck, you are sprinting hard to receive the next pass for a fast-break attack.

⚡ Speed Control during a Breakout

  1. Explode to your position: When the D-man gets the puck, explode at 100% speed to get to your breakout spot ahead of the opposing defenders.
  2. Glide and Present: Once you reach your spot, slow down to 50% or glide while facing the puck so you can catch the pass cleanly.
  3. Explode out of the zone: The split second the puck hits your stick, shift back into 100% full speed to attack the neutral zone before the other team can regroup.

Hockey Sense, Explosive Skating & Edge Work

At the U11 Rep A1 level, the best players are not just faster — they are smarter with their feet. Great centermen drive play, wingers attack space, and every forward needs controlled acceleration, tight turns, and deceptive body language.

Centers: the engine

Centers need to read the play before the puck arrives, control pace, support the D-man, and find the soft areas between defenders. They are the rhythm setters.

Wingers: the wall & the spear

Wingers attack the wall, create separation off the cycle, and attack the weak side with speed. They also need to be ready to dismantle a defense with sudden changes of direction.

2nd Period

What evaluators are actually scoring

Skill is close to equal among the players left at this stage. What separates them is habits — the things a player does without the puck.

Compete & battles

First to loose pucks. Wins the 1-on-1 along the boards. Strong body position before reaching in.

Pace & energy

Feet moving at full speed in both directions. No gliding, no coasting back into position.

Puck movement

Head up to scan the ice. Quick, clean passes to an open teammate instead of blind plays into traffic.

Defensive awareness

Hard backchecks all the way into the zone. Stays on the defensive side, between the man and the net.

Puck protection

Shield it, don't show it

Use your body to keep the puck away from the defender on the boards. Win the 50/50 battle, then drive it toward the danger areas — not back to the perimeter.

Forecheck

Take smart angles

First forward in forces the defenseman into a rushed decision. Second forward reads the turn and closes the gap fast to create the turnover.

Support

Be a real outlet

Come low into your own zone so your D has a clean passing option — don't leave the zone early looking for a breakaway pass.

Net front

Live in the blue paint

Stop in front of the net for screens, deflections, and rebounds. Skating past it on a wide loop doesn't create anything.

Backcheck

Sprint back, every time

The instant the puck is lost, turn and track through the middle of the ice. Every forward backchecks — centers go deepest, wingers own their side.

Rookie Report

Stories for a first U11 season

This is Ryan's first year at U11 — bigger ice, faster games, older competition. A few true-to-life stories other first-year players have lived through, and the lesson each one teaches.

The Big Ice Problem

The kid who skated the whole rink

A first-year U11 winger kept chasing the puck coast to coast every shift, the way he did on the smaller U9 sheet. By the second period his legs were gone, and the plays he used to make weren't there anymore.

His coach didn't tell him to slow down — he told him to shift shorter. Forty-five seconds, all-out, then off. The next game he had more jump in the third period than the first.

Lesson: full-ice hockey rewards short, explosive shifts — not covering the most ground.
The Confidence Dip

The kid who stopped shooting

Everyone has a game at U11 where the passes that used to work don't, and the bounces don't go your way. One first-year forward went silent after a rough first game — stopped shooting, started passing up open looks.

What got him unstuck wasn't a tip about his shot. It was one shift where he just went to the net anyway, expecting nothing, and got a rebound goal. Confidence came back from doing, not thinking.

Lesson: a bad shift doesn't need fixing mid-game — it needs the next shift, played the same way as always.
The Speed Gap

The kid who got beat to every puck

Older, stronger players in the same birth-year group can just be faster to loose pucks. A first-year defenseman kept losing races to the puck in his own corner and started reaching in with his stick instead of his feet.

The fix was positioning, not speed: get there early with good angling instead of racing in late. Once he started reading the play a half-second sooner, the "speed gap" mostly disappeared.

Lesson: you can't out-skate everyone at U11 — but you can out-think a lot of them.
The Bench Habit

The kid nobody noticed at first

A quiet first-year player wasn't scoring much through tryouts, but coaches kept writing his name down anyway. On the bench he was always the first one cheering a teammate's goal, first one sliding down to make room, never sulking after a bad shift.

He made the team. Not instead of a more skilled kid — alongside one who got cut for being a project to manage on the bench.

Lesson: at tryouts, how you act when you're not on the ice is being watched just as closely as when you are.

3rd Period

Habits that count — and game day

Small, repeatable choices decide close calls between two similar players.

Do this

  • ✓ Hard backcheck through the middle to strip attackers
  • ✓ Stop at the net front to screen and hunt rebounds
  • ✓ Take quick shifts, sprint to the bench
  • ✓ Battle in the corners with real lower-body leverage
  • ✓ Make simple, accurate passes to open teammates
  • ✓ Shrug off a mistake and get back to work

Not that

  • ✕ Gliding back or watching the play after a turnover
  • ✕ Curling away early on wide loops near the net
  • ✕ Long 90-second shifts that end in coasting
  • ✕ Reaching in with one hand instead of engaging
  • ✕ Trying to deke through three guys to show off
  • ✕ Showing frustration or bad body language on the bench
